The same goes for conventional protection mechanisms allowing creditors either to terminate the contractual relationship (such as non-transferability, *intuitu personae,* acceler- ation, change of control and prior approval clauses), or simply to adjust the pre-existing contractual relationship (such as jurisdiction clauses and hardship clauses). In conclusion, effective creditor protection will necessarily have to be based on an in-depth defence strategy consisting of four successive lines of defence which are gradually becoming more intrusive from the point of view of freedom of establishment:

- The first line of defence consists in contractual mechanisms. The study of these mechanisms has shown that they are both highly flexible and very effective for vigilant creditors. However, to ensure their effectiveness, it will also be necessary to establish a n institutional protection mechanism through extensive disclosure requirements. The latter will enable creditors to react in a timely manner and, where appropriate, to avail themselves of their contractual prerogatives before the division has taken effect. However, this first line of defence is far from being sufficient to guarantee adequate protection of creditors’ interests as it will only benefits vigilant creditors who are aware of their claim, who are able to assess the impact of a cross-border transaction on the repayment capacity of their debtor and who have sufficient bargaining power vis-à-vis the debtor to enable them to adapt the terms of their claim accordingly.
- The second line of defence, which will also be the main line of resist- ance, will revolve around the individual protection mechanism of claiming adequate guarantees. This mechanism, advocated both by national and European legislators and by the ECJ, allows creditors to obtain additional guarantees if the company cannot credibly demonstrate that the trans- action does not jeopardise the recovery of their debt and that the rele- vant creditors do not yet benefit from satisfactory guarantees. It appears to be an effective way of safeguarding the interests of creditors while respecting the balance of interests and the proportionality requirement of the Gebhard test, provided that it does not have suspensive effect. This specific mechanism, which will intervene *ex ante* for a period of three months from the publication of the joint draft, will necessarily have to demonstrate a certain flexibility through two simple presumptions that the transaction does not harm creditors, i.e. (i) in the presence of a con- clusive expert report and (ii) if a payment right of equivalent quality was granted to the relevant creditor, in order to avoid the collateral damage that would be caused by a prohibition of a potentially mutually beneficial or harmless transaction. The flanks will be guarded by more general civil law mechanisms, among which the *actio pauliana* will rank first.
