While capable of being applied to cross-border divisions, the study of the limits inherent to this other fundamental freedom both in a purely European context and in a situation also involving third countries has shown that it is of no practical interest. It follows that the negative harmonisation achieved by the ECJ through its case law on freedom of establishment is not, on its own, capable of making cross-border divisions a reality within the European Union, but must necessarily be accompanied by positive legal measures adopted either (i) by the various national legislators and articulated through specific rules of private international law or (ii) by the European legislator.

2.4. *Theories of private international law and national initiatives* Regarding the first alternative, a study of the various theories of private inter- national law developed in connection with cross-border divisions quickly reveal their limitations. However, it is clear that the close proximity between mergers and divisions means that the cross-border division procedure will necessarily be based to a very large extent on the regime applicable to cross-border mergers, whilst complying with the specific features of a division which, unlike a merger, is fundamentally characterised by a plurality of beneficiary companies and a single transferring company. However, in order to do so, and if cross-border divisions are indeed to be governed on the basis of the regime applicable to cross-border mergers, it will first be necessary to make their recognition by Member States mandatory in a purely domestic context in order to be able to structure the regime of cross-border divisions on the basis of the principle of the subsidiary application of the regime applicable to domestic divisions. Moreover, it is not conceivable to rely exclusively on national legislators in this area for two distinct reasons. Firstly, national approaches to cross-border divi- sions are likely to diverge significantly from one Member State to another and to generate frictions. This is easily demonstrated by the study of the Belgian and Luxembourg regimes. Second, some Member States which are less benev- olent to cross-border mobility would undoubtedly refrain from introducing any regime altogether.
