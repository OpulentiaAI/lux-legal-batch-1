2.5. *The new regime of cross-border divisions by incorporation of companies* In consideration of these shortcomings, it was only logical for the European legislator to adopt the Mobility Directive introducing for the first time a proce- dure for cross-border divisions by incorporation of new companies. While this Directive certainly represents a significant step towards the establishment of a European regime for cross-border divisions, it is not without drawbacks and suffers from four major shortcomings, i.e. (i) the absence of prior harmonisation of national divisions and the general inconsistencies resulting therefrom, (ii) a *ratione personae* scope limited to limited liability companies and depriving cer- tain companies in a precarious situation of a potentially effective reorganisation mechanism and a *ratione materiae* scope excluding all cross-border divisions involving more than one pre-existing company, (iii) a vague anti-abuse provision which could easily lead to divergent and possibly abusive practices by more pro- tectionist Member States and (iv) unjustified differences in treatment between cross-border mergers and divisions, to the disadvantage of the latter. These shortcomings, combined (i)  with the lessons learned from the cross-border mergers experience showing that a high level of legal certainty resulting from a Directive making it possible to significantly reduce both the costs and the time required to carry them out acts as a catalyst for cross-border operations, (ii) the inadequacy of indirect methods to achieve a result identical to that of a divi- sion by acquisition, (iii) the deterrent effect of the continuous legal uncertainty affecting divisions falling outside the scope of the Mobility Directive and (iv) the need for adequate protection of stakeholders, render a further intervention by the European legislator in the field of cross-border divisions both desirable and unavoidable. The restrictive approach of the European Commission towards cross-border divisions in the context of the Mobility Directive is therefore diffi- cult to understand. One possible explanation could be that the EU Commission deliberately chose to limit its first legislative initiative to the categories of divi- sions least likely to give rise to apprehension in mobility-averse Member States with a view to establishing that cross-border divisions represent an economically important restructuring method which is important for the internal market by relying on a significant increase in such operations following the national trans- position of the Mobility Directive. Irrespective of the Commission’s potentially hidden agenda, a European initiative in this area is unavoidable.
2.6. *The future of cross-border division regulation in the EU* A new initiative in the field of cross-border divisions will necessarily take the form of a Directive insofar as (i)  art.  50 TFEU imposes this form to gradually remove barriers to freedom of establishment and to ensure effective protection of stakeholders in this context, (ii) many restructuring procedures are already regulated in a Directive, (iii)  the lightning legislative process of the Mobility Directive has sufficiently demonstrated the political acceptability of proceeding by this legislative method to regulate and strengthen cross-border mobility of companies within the EU. However, the intrinsic shortcomings of a Directive, such as the need to reach a political compromise leaving sufficient leeway to national legislators and the excessive complexity of such an operation due
