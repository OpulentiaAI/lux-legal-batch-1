2. *Immediate parties’ perspective on cross-border divisions*
2.1. *Economic interest of divisions* The attention given to this issue is fully warranted in light of the economic interest of cross-border divisions. Qualifying as a divestment operation, they represent a particularly flexible mechanism that can be used both as part of a defensive strategy aimed at preventing a devaluation of the company and as part of a more offensive strategy aimed at creating value. For example, a divi- sion will allow a company to refocus on its *core business* by correcting an error of judgement, eliminate negative synergies or carry out a genuine strategic reorientation dictated in particular by an economic recession or the disappoint- ing financial results of a declining industry. They also make it possible to limit the effects of the diversification discount from which conglomerates tend to suffer, to correct a possible asymmetry of information existing between the company’s managers and the external capital market, or to meet the capital needs of a promising branch of activity or of the conglomerate itself. A cross-border divi- sion may also serve as a defence against hostile takeover bids or fulfil certain purely legal objectives such as adapting to the requirements of competition law, benefitting from a more advantageous tax or regulatory regime or limiting the risks of civil liability arising from certain activities. The economic analysis of divi- sions also shows that while the increase in *shareholder value* is at the centre of the decision, the transaction will not necessarily be detrimental to other stake- holders and even be beneficial for them. Furthermore, a cross-border division will, in order to be sustainable in the long run, also have to take into account the interests of such stakeholders. Despite being potentially very advantageous, divisions are not infallible. For example, the company or companies resulting from the transaction may prove to be too small and therefore suffer from a liquidity discount or, on the contrary, be more exposed to the risks of a hostile takeover bid. A division may also make the resulting entities less attractive to investors as their activities will be less diversified. However, its main drawbacks remain the complexity of its implementation and the length of its preparation. An effective regime for cross-border divisions should therefore (i) on the one hand, take into consideration the interests of the stakeholders while favouring transactions that are beneficial to both majority shareholders and creditors, minority shareholders and employees and (ii)  on the other hand, reduce the costs, duration and complexity of such a transaction to the absolute minimum.
2.2. *Legal nature and main characteristics* The implementation of such a scheme firstly requires a precise definition of the operation itself. A demerger, of polymorphous legal nature, may be analysed as a restructuring operation which may take the form of either a *sui* *generis* contract or a unilateral legal act and is characterised in principle by
