3.4. *The challenges of minority shareholders protection* Although less salient than the issue of creditor protection, the question of the protection of minority shareholders in the context of a cross-border division nonetheless requires an adequate response. Minority shareholders are exposed to up to five distinct risks in a cross-border division, three of which (the risk of an inadequate exchange ratio, the risk of being forced to undertake a transaction by the majority shareholders and the risk of economic failure) are common to the shareholders of both the divided company and the beneficiary companies and two of which (the risk of asymmetrical divisions and the risk linked to the cross-border nature of the transaction) are specific to the shareholders of the divided company. The first challenge of an effective minority shareholders pro- tection regime is therefore to adequately address these risks to avoid a negative impact on the company’s access to capital. The second challenge is to achieve a double balance. Firstly, an effective regime must neither allow majority share- holders to spoliate minority shareholders nor constitute too great an obstacle to the completion of a transaction that is economically indispensable to the continuation of the underlying business. Secondly, it must neither lead to an excessive erosion of the assets or cash of the relevant company (which could be potentially be harmful to creditors), nor unduly sacrifice the interests of minority shareholders who may be forced to undertake a cross-border division against their will. The third challenge will ensure that any protective mechanism com- plies with the conditions of the so-called “*Gebhard test*”. The fourth and final challenge will be to take into account the fact that, unlike creditors, minority shareholders incur an additional significant risk due to the cross-border nature of the transaction insofar as it entails a change in the *lex societatis*.
3.5. *The contours of an effective minority shareholders protection regime* The analysis of the protective mechanisms introduced by the European leg- islator through the double prism of freedom of establishment and protection of creditors’ interests while comparing them to the mechanisms of two juris- dictions that are less protective of minority shareholders’ interests, namely Belgium and Luxembourg, and two jurisdictions with some of the most elabo- rate regimes in this area, namely Germany and Austria, has led to the conclusion that the regime advocated by the European legislator in the framework of the Mobility Directive remains relatively proportionate and even goes, to some extent, in the direction of enhanced cross-border mobility.
