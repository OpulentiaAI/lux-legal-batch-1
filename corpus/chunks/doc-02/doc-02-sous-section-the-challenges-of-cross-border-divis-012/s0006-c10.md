In addition, the a*ctio pauliana*, while being widely represented in the Member States, (i)  enjoys a certain flexibility in that the third party concerned may avail itself of the benefit of discussion or dismiss it by simply paying the creditor, (ii) prevents any competition with the creditors of the third party accomplice and (iii) may be brought against a third party sub-acquirer in the case of successive divisions. On the flank level, hardship combined with a right of unilateral termination could be of significant use where the allocation of an adequate guarantee is not sufficient to protect a creditor. This line of defence is, like the first one, insufficient in that it is primarily aimed at creditors who are vigilant, aware of their claim and have sufficient means to protect their rights. Moreover, the conditions of both the *actio pauliana* and hardship hard to meet, thus reserving such protective mechanisms to more exceptional situations.

- The third line of defence is articulated around the joint and several lia- bility of the recipient companies for the debts of the divided company limited to the net assets transferred. This institutional mechanism par excellence will make it possible to overcome both the rational apathy obstacle and the information asymmetry existing between creditors and the relevant companies. Furthermore, it is capable both of effectively protecting involuntary creditors or those still unaware of the existence of their claim at the time of completion of the division and of compensating for the freedom enjoyed by the shareholders with regard to the distri- bution of the assets and liabilities of the divided company. However, it may only intervene in case of default of the company to which the claim was originally transferred and should also, in case of a partial division, benefit the creditors of the company being divided whose claim was not transferred in the operation.
- The final line of defence, which will only come into play if the first three lines have failed, will take full advantage of the functional interdependence between company law and insolvency law, and will consist of mechanisms under insolvency law. This last line of resistance will be characterised by a disruption of the balance in favour of creditors as well as by a particularly intrusive character from the point of view of freedom of establishment taking full advantage of both the ECJ case-law in the *Kornhaas* judgment¹³ and the safeguards deployed by the Insolvency Regulation¹⁴. This defensive strategy should not, however, be uniformly implemented
through exhaustive harmonisation, but will require a certain flexibility to adapt to the very different backgrounds of the general creditor protection regimes of the various Member States and to the fact that the mechanisms of which they are composed transcend company law alone and involve other branches of law
