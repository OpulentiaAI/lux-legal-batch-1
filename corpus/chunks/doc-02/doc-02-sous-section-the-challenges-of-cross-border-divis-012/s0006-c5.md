2.7. *The cornerstones of a European cross-border division regime* The ambiguous and restrictive attitude of the European legislator towards cross-border divisions raises certain questions and one can only wonder whether, from the outset, the European Commission, sensing the reluctance of certain Member States, did not make the strategic choice to proceed in two successive stages to make cross-border divisions a reality within the European Union. In other words, it is quite possible that, from the Commission’s perspective, the need to proceed with the harmonisation of cross-border divisions involving more than one pre-existing company has never been in doubt, but the best way it found to overcome any reluctance by Member States and to adapt to the skepticism of trade unions towards them was to proceed with a two-steps approach. The first step would consist in an asymmetrical harmonisation while at the same time accustoming Member States to divisions by incorporation of new companies, which are in essence less contentious, and the second step would consist in a more thorough harmonisation exercise of divisions. On this basis, it is worth examining the cross-border division regime of the Mobility Directive to determine to what extent it would also be compatible with both mixed cross-border divisions and divisions by acquisition. The exercise will consist both (i) in an identification of the indications supporting the fact that the Mobility Directive is only a preliminary step to a broader initiative and (ii) in an attempt to resolve possible incompatibilities by taking full advantage of the cross-border division regimes found in Belgian and Luxembourg law, as well as elements of comparison with German, Austrian, French and Italian law. This detailed analysis of the seven steps of the general European model for restructuring (namely (i) the common draft terms of division, (ii) the report(s) of the management or administrative body, (iii) the expert’s report, (iv) the pub- licity measures, (v) the approval of the general meeting, (vi) the double legality control and (vii) the registration and entry into effect) on which the division by incorporation regime enshrined in the Mobility Directive is based, reveals that this regime is also generally compatible with the specific features of a cross-bor- der division involving more than one existing company. However, it would be erroneous to conclude that a mere extension of the *ratione materiae* scope of the Mobility Directive would suffice to make cross-border divisions a reality within the European Union.
